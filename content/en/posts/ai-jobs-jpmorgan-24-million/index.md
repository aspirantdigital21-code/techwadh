**24 million jobs in five years** is the number making headlines. In J.P. Morgan Wealth Management’s *Outlook 2027*, it is the result of a **global scenario for knowledge-worker roles**, based on specific assumptions about how businesses spend on AI. One way it could show up is through jobs companies would otherwise have filled. That makes hiring decisions as important to the story as layoffs. [J.P. Morgan Outlook 2027, page 27](https://www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpmorgan/documents/wealth-management/outlook-2027.pdf#page=27)

## Where the 24 million comes from

| Input | J.P. Morgan’s illustrative calculation |
| --- | ---: |
| Potential enterprise AI spending in the early 2030s | About $2.4 trillion |
| Share assumed to replace roles that would otherwise be filled | 50%, about $1.2 trillion |
| Effective cost of AI per role in the calculation | $50,000, about half the assumed fully loaded worker cost |
| Result over five years | About **24 million roles worldwide** |
| Annual share of the global knowledge workforce | About **1.3%** |

The calculation divides $1.2 trillion by $50,000 and spreads the resulting 24 million roles across five years. Both spending and the share devoted to replacing work are assumptions. Using more of the budget to help workers do their jobs, or paying more for AI per role, changes the result. [Report methodology, footnote 28](https://www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpmorgan/documents/wealth-management/outlook-2027.pdf#page=27)

## Why job openings matter as much as layoffs

Imagine a team that once planned to hire another analyst to assemble weekly reports. If software handles the first draft and an existing analyst checks the numbers, the team may drop the new vacancy. The work has changed and a potential hire has disappeared. A layoff, where an existing employee loses their position, is a different outcome.

J.P. Morgan also sees changes **within** jobs as more likely than entire roles disappearing. Drafting text, extracting fields or proposing code can be delegated in some settings. Checking accuracy, handling exceptions and taking responsibility still have to be assigned to someone. A separate 2025 ILO study estimated that one in four workers globally were in occupations with some generative AI exposure. That study measures potential exposure, so its figure cannot be read as a count of job losses or added to J.P. Morgan’s scenario. [J.P. Morgan report](https://www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpmorgan/documents/wealth-management/outlook-2027.pdf#page=27) · [ILO research brief](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/Research%20brief_GenAI%202025%20Update.pdf)

## What it means for India

The report provides **no India-specific estimate for this scenario**. Its roughly **16 million U.S. roles** figure is another illustration, conditional on two-thirds of the global impact falling in the United States. Indian readers can use the global story to ask more useful questions about their own roles. Which tasks repeat every week? Which decisions require local knowledge, customer context or review? Where might a company change the work it hires for? [J.P. Morgan Outlook 2027](https://www.jpmorgan.com/content/dam/jpmorgan/documents/wealth-management/outlook-2027.pdf#page=27)

## A quick exercise for students and office workers

1. Pick one recurring task, such as preparing meeting notes, summarising public research or sorting sample support requests.
2. Trial an AI-assisted version with public or synthetic data and compare it with your normal process.
3. Record time saved, errors you caught and the decisions you still made yourself. Keep that comparison as a portfolio example.
